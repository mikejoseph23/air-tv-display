Air TV Display

Turn a spare Mac into a wireless second display for your main Mac.

A good screen shouldn't go to waste

Plenty of us have an older Mac sitting nearby with a perfectly good display, like a 27 inch Intel iMac. It would make a great second screen for the Mac you actually work on. The trouble is that macOS won't let you use it that way.

Target Display Mode is gone

It only ever worked with iMacs from 2009 through mid 2014, over a Thunderbolt or Mini DisplayPort cable, and only when both Macs run older versions of macOS (High Sierra on the iMac, Catalina or earlier on the other Mac).

AirPlay Receiver needs a newer Mac

Apple's built-in AirPlay to Mac requires macOS Monterey or later and, as the receiver, an iMac Pro, a Mac mini from 2020 or later, or another Mac from 2018 or later. Older Macs don't get the option at all.

Air TV Display fills the gap

It turns any Mac running macOS 13 or later into an AirPlay receiver. Your main Mac sees it in Screen Mirroring, just like an Apple TV, and can use it as a real second display.

How it works

Open Air TV Display on the spare Mac and it goes full screen and waits. It announces itself on your local network the same way an Apple TV does, so your main Mac needs nothing extra installed. Choose it from Control Center and macOS streams a display to it over the network.

Extend or mirror

Use it as a separate display you can drag windows onto, or mirror your main screen. macOS handles the arrangement like any other monitor.

Sound included

Audio from the stream plays through the spare Mac's speakers, kept in sync with the picture. Turn it off in Settings if you'd rather not.

Hardware decoding

Video is decoded by the Mac's own media engine through VideoToolbox and drawn with Metal, so it stays smooth and light on the processor.

No cables, no PIN

Nothing to plug in and no code to type. Pick it from the list and it connects.

Nothing on the sender

It speaks standard AirPlay, so the Mac you're sending from uses the Screen Mirroring it already has.

Keeps itself current

New versions arrive through Sparkle, signed and verified before they install.

Getting started

  1. Download the DMG on the spare Mac, open it, and drag Air TV Display into Applications.
  2. Open it. Allow local network access when macOS asks. The app fills the screen and waits for a connection.
  3. On your main Mac, open Control Center, click Screen Mirroring, and choose Air TV Display.
  4. Choose “Use As Separate Display” to extend your desktop, or pick mirroring if you want the same picture on both.
  5. Adjust it any time: right-click the screen or press ⌘, to open Settings, where you can rename the receiver, turn audio on or off, and launch it at login.

Requirements

  • Receiving Mac macOS 13 Ventura or later, Apple silicon or Intel. This is the spare Mac that runs Air TV Display.
  • Sending Mac A Mac with Screen Mirroring in Control Center. Designed and tested Mac to Mac.
  • Network Both Macs on the same local network. Ethernet on both gives the lowest latency; Wi-Fi works too.

Questions

How much lag is there?

Enough that you'll notice it when dragging windows quickly, little enough that reading, writing, chat, documentation, dashboards and video all feel fine. Most of the delay comes from the sending Mac capturing and encoding its screen, which no receiver can remove. A wired connection on both Macs helps the most.

What resolution do I get?

macOS streams to AirPlay receivers like this one at 1920×1080. On a large Retina screen that's softer than native, which is the tradeoff for doing it over the network. It works best as a reference or secondary screen rather than your main workspace.

Does audio work?

Yes. Sound from the stream plays on the receiving Mac, synchronized with the video. You can switch it off in Settings if you want sound to stay on your main Mac.

Which Macs can I use?

Any Mac that runs macOS 13 or later can be the receiver, Intel or Apple silicon. It's most useful on Macs that can't use Apple's built-in AirPlay Receiver, such as a 2017 iMac. The sender is your everyday Mac. It's designed around Mac to Mac; iPhone and iPad use the same protocol and may work, but Mac is what it's built and tested for.

Is it private and secure?

The stream goes directly between your two Macs over your local network and never touches the internet. There's no account, no sign-in and no analytics. The only outside connection is the update check to iadev.net, which you can turn off in Settings. Like an Apple TV without a passcode, anyone on the same network can connect while it's running, so quit it when you're not using it on a shared network.

Does it cost anything?

No. It's free and open source under the GNU GPL version 3.

Open source

The full source is on GitHub under the GNU General Public License v3. The AirPlay protocol work, including discovery, pairing and the streaming session, comes from the excellent open source UxPlay project. Air TV Display wraps that library in a native Mac app with VideoToolbox decoding, Metal rendering and Core Audio playback. Updates are delivered with Sparkle.
